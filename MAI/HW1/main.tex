%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
We re-implement SimSiam~\cite{chen2021simsiam} with a ResNet-18 backbone and pretrain it on the 100k unlabeled images of STL-10.
With only 1,000 labeled images, a linear probe on the frozen SimSiam encoder reaches $80.98\pm0.35\%$ test accuracy (200 epochs, 3 pretraining seeds), while the same network trained from scratch reaches only $47.74\pm1.17\%$.
We also reproduce the complete collapse reported in the original paper: removing either the stop-gradient or the predictor drops the linear probe to exactly chance level (10.00\%).
As a new ablation, we remove one family of augmentations at a time and find that the model degrades most severely without cropping.
Without cropping, SimSiam does not collapse to a point but suffers \emph{dimensional collapse}: the backbone's effective rank falls from 318 to 6.7, and accuracy \emph{drops} with longer training.
Replacing the crop with simpler operations shows that the key is the \emph{difference between the two views}: sharing one random crop between both views is as bad as no cropping, while independent random masking recovers two thirds of the gap.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Self-supervised learning aims to learn visual representations from unlabeled images so that downstream tasks need only a few labels.
SimSiam~\cite{chen2021simsiam} is one of the simplest methods of this kind. It trains a Siamese network to make the representations of two augmented views of an image agree, and uses no negative pairs, no large batches and no momentum encoder.
Its central claim is that a stop-gradient operation together with a predictor head is enough to keep the network from collapsing to a constant output.

Because SimSiam has no negative pairs, the difference between the two augmented views is its \emph{only} learning signal.
The original paper studies the architecture and the loss in detail, but it does not ablate the augmentations that create this signal.
In this report we re-implement SimSiam on STL-10~\cite{coates2011stl10} and study both sides.
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item We show that SimSiam pretraining on 100k unlabeled images beats training from scratch by 33.2 points when only 1,000 labels are available (\cref{sec:exp_cls}).
\item We reproduce the complete collapse caused by removing the stop-gradient or the predictor (\cref{sec:exp_arch}).
\item We ablate the augmentations and show that without cropping, SimSiam does not collapse to a point but suffers \emph{dimensional collapse}, which worsens with longer training (\cref{sec:exp_aug}).
\item We decompose the crop and find that what matters is a content difference between the two views, not data diversity (\cref{sec:exp_crop}).
\item We show that neither the loss value nor the output std detects this failure, while the effective rank and a properly scaled linear probe do (\cref{sec:exp_pretrain,sec:exp_aug,sec:exp_length}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Siamese self-supervised learning.}
Contrastive methods such as SimCLR~\cite{chen2020simclr} pull two views of the same image together and push views of different images apart. The negative pairs prevent the trivial constant solution.
BYOL~\cite{grill2020byol} removes the negatives and instead uses a predictor and a momentum encoder.
SimSiam~\cite{chen2021simsiam} shows that the momentum encoder is also unnecessary: a stop-gradient and a predictor alone are sufficient.

\paragraph{Role of augmentation.}
SimCLR~\cite{chen2020simclr} found that the combination of random cropping and color distortion is the most important augmentation pair for contrastive learning.
In that setting the negatives provide a separate force against collapse. Whether the same holds for SimSiam, where augmentation is the only source of signal, has not been studied in the original paper.

\paragraph{Collapse.}
\emph{Complete collapse} maps every input to the same vector. \emph{Dimensional collapse}~\cite{jing2022dimensional} is a milder failure: the embeddings stay spread out but occupy only a low-dimensional subspace.
The output std monitored in SimSiam detects the former but not the latter. We therefore also measure the effective rank~\cite{roy2007erank} of the embeddings.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{SimSiam}
Given an image $x$, two random augmentations produce views $x_1$ and $x_2$.
An encoder $f$, consisting of a backbone and a projection MLP, maps each view to $z_i=f(x_i)$, and a prediction MLP $h$ maps it to $p_i=h(z_i)$.
With the negative cosine similarity $\mathcal{D}(p,z)=-\frac{p}{\|p\|_2}\cdot\frac{z}{\|z\|_2}$, the symmetric loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\tfrac12\mathcal{D}(p_1,\text{sg}(z_2))+\tfrac12\mathcal{D}(p_2,\text{sg}(z_1)),
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\text{sg}(\cdot)$ is the stop-gradient. The minimum of $\mathcal{L}$ is $-1$.

We use a ResNet-18 backbone~\cite{he2016resnet} with its fc layer removed (512-d output), a 3-layer projection MLP ($512\!\to\!2048\!\to\!2048\!\to\!2048$, BN on every layer) and a bottleneck prediction MLP ($2048\!\to\!512\!\to\!2048$).

\subsection{Ablations}
\paragraph{Architecture.}
Following Sec.~4.1--4.2 of the original paper, we (a) remove the stop-gradient so that gradients flow through both branches, and (b) replace the predictor $h$ with the identity.

\paragraph{Augmentation.}
The original paper's EM-like hypothesis (Sec.~5) views SimSiam as approximately solving
\begin{equation}
\min_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{x,\mathcal{T}}\bigl\|\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathcal{T}(x))-\eta_x\bigr\|^2,
\label{eq:em}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_x$ is the expected representation of $x$ over augmentations $\mathcal{T}$.
If the augmentations are too weak, this expectation becomes trivial.
To test this, we remove one family of augmentations at a time:
\textbf{no\_color} drops color jitter and grayscale, and \textbf{no\_crop} drops RandomResizedCrop, so that both views show the full image and differ only in flip and color.

\paragraph{Decomposing the crop.}
RandomResizedCrop changes two things at once: each view shows only \emph{part} of the content, and that part is \emph{rescaled and shifted} to fill the frame. It also makes the two views of an image differ, while adding diversity across epochs.
To separate these factors, we replace the crop in the full pipeline (flip and color are kept) with:
\textbf{crop\_in\_place} keeps the crop region at its original position and scale and fills the rest with the dataset mean (partial content only);
\textbf{zoom\_out} shrinks the whole image and pastes it at a random position (rescaling and shifting only);
\textbf{mask} drops random 16-pixel patches, keeping the same area fraction as the crop (partial content, scattered, no rescaling);
\textbf{same\_crop} applies one random crop to \emph{both} views, so the data diversity matches the baseline but the views no longer differ in crop.

\subsection{Measuring Collapse}
Besides the std of the $\ell_2$-normalized output, which approaches $1/\sqrt{d}$ for outputs spread over the unit sphere and $0$ under complete collapse, we measure the effective rank (erank)~\cite{roy2007erank}.
Let $\sigma_k$ be the singular values of the centered, $\ell_2$-normalized features of the 8,000 test images. Then
\begin{equation}
\text{erank}=\exp\Bigl(-\sum_k p_k\log p_k\Bigr),\quad p_k=\frac{\sigma_k}{\sum_j\sigma_j}.
\end{equation}
We focus on the 512-d backbone feature, which is the input to the linear probe.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We use STL-10~\cite{coates2011stl10} at $96\times96$. Pretraining uses the 100k unlabeled images.
Classifiers train on the official fold~0 (1,000 images). Validation uses 500 held-out, label-balanced training images, and testing uses all 8,000 test images.

\paragraph{Pretraining.}
We use SGD (momentum 0.9, weight decay $5\!\times\!10^{-4}$) with batch size 512 and $\text{lr}=0.03\times512/256$, with a 10-epoch warmup followed by cosine decay.
The augmentation follows the original paper: RandomResizedCrop (scale 0.2--1), horizontal flip, color jitter $(0.4,0.4,0.4,0.1)$ with $p{=}0.8$, and grayscale with $p{=}0.2$.
A kNN monitor ($k{=}200$, $\tau{=}0.1$) uses fold~0 as the memory bank and evaluates on the validation images after every epoch.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
The \emph{linear probe} trains a linear layer on the frozen backbone with SGD (lr 30, no weight decay) for 50 epochs. The \emph{BN probe} adds a parameter-free BN before the linear layer, with lr chosen on validation.
The from-scratch baseline (\emph{SFT}) trains the whole ResNet-18 with the pretraining optimizer settings for 50 epochs.
Every reported test accuracy uses the checkpoint with the best validation accuracy.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
For the 200-epoch comparisons we pretrain with seeds 123/456/789, and linear-probe each model with 5 finetuning seeds (12/34/56/78/90).
The \emph{pretraining seed} is the unit of replication: we report the mean $\pm$ std over the 3 per-model means and compare settings with two-sided Welch $t$-tests.
We also compute macro one-vs-rest AUROC and macro F1 for every probe, and check every comparison under all three metrics with Holm correction.
When a setting has only one pretraining run, its $\pm$ reflects finetuning noise only, and we say so.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
All experiments ran on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti (16\,GB) with CUDA 12.8, Python 3.10.21, PyTorch 2.8.0 and torchvision 0.23.0 (\cref{fig:env}).
One pretraining epoch takes about 45\,s; a linear probe or SFT run takes 1--2\,min.
The code extends the course starter code: \code{model.py} (SimSiam, with \code{stop\_grad} and \code{predictor} flags), \code{data.py} (two-view augmentation with an \code{aug} option), \code{pretrain.py}, \code{linear\_probe.py}, \code{sft.py} and \code{utils.py}, plus analysis scripts for plotting, effective rank and statistical tests.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

Absolute numbers are not comparable to the original paper, which uses ImageNet, ResNet-50 and more labels, so we compare only qualitative trends.

\subsection{SimSiam Pretraining}
\label{sec:exp_pretrain}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{figures/pretrain_loss.png}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{figures/pretrain_knn.png}
\caption{SimSiam pretraining for 100 epochs: training loss (left) and kNN validation accuracy (right).}
\label{fig:pretrain}

\Cref{fig:pretrain} shows a 100-epoch pretraining run.
The loss falls quickly from $-0.15$ to about $-0.78$ during the 10-epoch warmup and ends at $-0.848$, while the kNN accuracy rises steadily to 73.4\%.
The std of the normalized projector output ends at $0.02195$, close to $1/\sqrt{2048}=0.02210$, so the model has not collapsed.

Between epochs 50 and 80 the loss stays flat and even rises slightly ($-0.843\rightarrow-0.837$), while the kNN accuracy climbs from 60.6\% to about 70\%.
The SimSiam loss only measures agreement between two views, so its value says little about how useful the features are. We return to this point in \cref{sec:exp_aug}.

Both classifiers use the same ResNet-18 and the same 1,000 labeled images (\cref{fig:cls}, \cref{tab:cls}).
The linear probe trains only a $512\!\times\!10$ layer on frozen features. It passes 70\% validation accuracy around epoch 10 and reaches a much lower training loss.
SFT stays near chance for the first 13 epochs and plateaus around 45\%.

The gap is not caused by undertrained baselines. Training SFT longer saturates at about 49--50\% (100 epochs: 49.62\%; 200 epochs: 49.01\%, with training loss 0.24, so it overfits), and a 200-epoch linear probe adds only $+0.8$ points over 50 epochs.
SimSiam therefore learns from the 100k unlabeled images what 1,000 labels alone cannot teach.

\paragraph{Error analysis.}
In \cref{fig:pred} and the confusion matrix (included in the code submission), the errors are mostly confusions between visually similar animals: cat$\leftrightarrow$dog, dog$\leftrightarrow$horse, monkey$\rightarrow$cat/dog and deer$\leftrightarrow$horse.
Vehicles (car, ship, truck, airplane) reach 86--93\% per-class accuracy.
Some mistakes come from context: a sailing ship in front of a building is labeled ``cat'', and an airplane against a blue sky is labeled ``bird''.
Augmentation-invariant features describe global shape and texture well but can be misled by the background.

\begin{table}[t]

\toprule
Setting & Loss & Std & kNN & Lin.\ probe \\
\midrule
Baseline & $-0.908$ & 0.021 & 77.6 & 81.35 \\
w/o stop-grad & $-1.000$ & 0 & 10.0 & 10.00 \\
w/o predictor & $-1.000$ & 0 & 13.2 & 10.00 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Architecture ablations after 200 epochs (final-epoch values; one run each). After 100 epochs, the two ablations reach 14.89\% and 16.16\% linear probe accuracy.}
\label{tab:arch}
\end{table}

Both variants reproduce the collapse reported in the original paper (\cref{fig:arch}, \cref{tab:arch}).
Within a few epochs the loss reaches its minimum of $-1$ and the output std drops toward 0, so every image is mapped to the same vector.
After 200 epochs the linear probe scores \emph{exactly} 10.00\% with training loss $\ln 10 = 2.303$, which is chance level for 10 classes. The original paper's 0.1\% is the same result for ImageNet's 1000 classes.
After 100 epochs the probes still reach about 15\%, because the backbone has not fully collapsed yet.

The collapse happens in two stages: in the first 5 epochs the std falls sharply to about 0.003, and between epochs 40 and 100 it decays slowly to 0.
The two ablations produce nearly the same curves, which matches the original paper's analysis. Without a predictor, \cref{eq:loss} has the same gradient as $\mathcal{D}(z_1,z_2)$, so removing the predictor in effect also removes the stop-gradient.
Neither the architecture nor BN prevents the trivial solution; the asymmetry from the stop-gradient and the predictor is required.
Because collapse here is deterministic, one run per variant is enough.

\paragraph{Lower loss, worse representation.}
Both variants reach a \emph{lower} loss than the baseline, because the views are easier to align, yet their accuracy is far lower (\cref{tab:aug}).
Removing color costs 30.6 points ($p{=}1.8\!\times\!10^{-4}$). Removing crop costs 56.2 points even under the BN probe ($p{=}1.3\!\times\!10^{-4}$), and no\_crop is 25.6 points below no\_color ($p{=}3.7\!\times\!10^{-5}$).
Cropping is therefore the more important augmentation. This agrees with SimCLR, but the drop is larger here: without negatives, a too-easy view pair leaves no other signal to learn from.
no\_crop is also the least stable setting: its std across pretraining seeds is about 6$\times$ the baseline's (F-test $p\approx0.025$, $n{=}3$).

\paragraph{The standard probe fails on no\_crop.}
With lr 30, the linear probe on no\_crop features never gets below training loss $\ln 10$, whether trained for 50, 100 or 200 epochs.
The no\_crop features occupy a tiny subspace with very small variance, so the fixed lr is poorly scaled for them.
The BN probe standardizes the features (validation selects lr 1.0, versus 0.03 for the baseline) and raises no\_crop to 24.77\% ($+7.27$, paired $t{=}19.2$, $p{=}0.003$).
We therefore report the BN probe for no\_crop; the features remain about 58 points below the baseline.

\paragraph{no\_crop gets worse with more training.}
no\_color improves from 100 to 200 epochs ($43.9\rightarrow50.4$), but no\_crop moves the other way.
With 3 seeded 100-epoch runs and the BN probe, its accuracy drops from $38.17\pm2.39$ to $24.77\pm1.54$ (Welch $t{=}8.18$, df 3.4, $p{=}0.0023$; 95\% CI of the change $[-18.3,-8.5]$).
Its kNN curve never rises steadily, unlike the baseline's (\cref{fig:aug}, left).

\paragraph{Dimensional collapse, not complete collapse.}
The effective rank explains these results (\cref{fig:aug}, right).
The 200-epoch no\_stop-grad and no\_predictor models are single points: their feature variance relative to the mean squared norm is $10^{-15}$ or smaller.
no\_crop is different. Its projector output $z$ still spreads out (relative variance 0.93, nonzero std), but occupies only about 4--8 of 2048 dimensions, and its backbone erank is just $6.7\pm2.8$, versus $317.5\pm2.4$ for the baseline.
This is \emph{dimensional collapse}~\cite{jing2022dimensional}.
It worsens with training: for every seed, the backbone erank drops from 100 to 200 epochs ($53.3\rightarrow9.2$, $120.3\rightarrow7.4$, $34.2\rightarrow3.6$) together with the accuracy.
It also explains why the standard probe failed: a few informative directions with tiny scale require a standardized head.
In terms of \cref{eq:em}, a crop-free pair differs only in color and flip. Averaging over such augmentations gives an $\eta_x$ that a few color-invariant directions can match, and the objective keeps shrinking the representation onto those directions.
\textbf{Stop-gradient and the predictor prevent complete collapse, but not dimensional collapse.}

\paragraph{Not overfitting.}
no\_crop does not memorize its training images: its SimSiam loss on 8,000 unseen images differs from that on 8,000 training images by at most 0.001, with the same backbone erank (\eg 9.0 vs.\ 9.1).
All comparisons above also remain significant after Holm correction under accuracy, macro AUROC and macro F1 ($p_\text{Holm}\le0.035$).

\subsection{What Does the Crop Contribute?}
\label{sec:exp_crop}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\setlength\tabcolsep{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Crop replacement & Loss & kNN & BN probe & AUROC \\
\midrule

\end{tabular}

\label{tab:crop}
\end{table}

\Cref{tab:crop} reports the variants of \cref{sec:method}, each pretrained once (seed 123) for 200 epochs.
Measured as the fraction of the no\_crop$\to$baseline gap they recover under the BN probe, masking recovers 67\%, crop\_in\_place 43\% and zoom\_out 24\%.

\paragraph{The views must differ, not just the data.}
same\_crop sees exactly as many distinct crops over training as the baseline, yet it is as poor as no\_crop (22.2\% vs.\ 25.4\%) with the same near-minimal loss ($-0.997$) and the same kNN accuracy (21.2\%).
Data diversity and sample count therefore do not explain the no\_crop failure. What matters is that the two views of one image differ in \emph{which content} they show.

\paragraph{Partial content and rescaling are complementary.}
Each component alone recovers only part of the gap: hiding content (crop\_in\_place) helps more than rescaling and shifting (zoom\_out), but neither comes close to the full crop, which combines both.
Scattered masking is the strongest single replacement, probably because it hides different parts of the whole object in each view instead of one contiguous region.

Longer schedules keep helping, with diminishing returns: 75.8\% (100 epochs), 81.0\% (200) and 86.7\% (1000), matching the trend in Tab.~4 of the original paper.
Within the 1000-epoch run, the loss and kNN accuracy plateau from epoch 300 to 500 while the LR is still above half its peak.
A saturation rule (less than 1 point gained per 100 epochs) would stop training at epoch 436, yet the linear probe accuracy keeps rising from 80\% to 87\% as the LR anneals.
The plateau is therefore limited by the LR, not by the representation; real saturation happens only in the last 100 epochs ($+0.07$ points from epoch 950 to 1000).
Snapshot accuracy also fluctuates: epoch 700 is 2.0 points \emph{below} epoch 650, far more than the std across probe seeds (at most 0.6), so a single mid-schedule snapshot does not reliably represent ``training up to that epoch''.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

(i) Erank is not a quality measure across settings. For no\_color, erank \emph{decreases} from 100 to 200 epochs ($224\rightarrow184$) while accuracy increases ($43.9\rightarrow50.4$). Our evidence for no\_crop is strong only because rank and accuracy fall together, and by large amounts.
(ii) A falling std is not a sign of collapse either. The 1000-epoch baseline's std halves to 0.0100, yet its backbone erank (392) and accuracy (86.7\%) are the highest of all our models.
(iii) We use $n{=}3$ pretraining seeds, so the Welch df is only 2--4 and the CIs are wide. Even so, every effect above is many standard deviations in size.
(iv) The crop-decomposition variants (\cref{sec:exp_crop}) have a single pretraining seed, so their ranking is only indicative, although the gaps between adjacent variants (11--17 points) far exceed the baseline's seed-to-seed std (0.35).

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
SimSiam on STL-10 turns 100k unlabeled images into features that beat training from scratch by 33 points with only 1,000 labels.
We confirmed that removing the stop-gradient or the predictor causes complete collapse.
Our augmentation ablation reveals a second failure mode: when the views are too similar (no cropping), SimSiam keeps a nonzero std and a lower loss, yet its features collapse onto a few dimensions and degrade with more training.
Neither the loss value nor the output std detects this; the effective rank and a properly scaled linear probe do.
Decomposing the crop shows that the views must differ in the content they show: a crop shared by both views is as harmful as no crop at all.
