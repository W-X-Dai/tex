\begin{abstract}
The phase ordering problem---determining the optimal sequence of compiler
optimization passes---is NP-hard and has resisted efficient exact solutions for
decades.  We present a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) system that learns
pass-ordering policies for the LLVM compiler using the CompilerGym
framework~\cite{CompilerGym}, without relying on large language models or
hand-crafted heuristics.
Our approach combines recurrent Proximal Policy Optimization
(PPO)~\cite{schulman2017proximal} with three key contributions:
(1)~a carefully designed state representation that augments Autophase IR
features~\cite{8735549} with per-feature online normalization and per-action
usage counts;
(2)~truncation-aware Generalized Advantage Estimation
(GAE)~\cite{schulman2015high} that correctly bootstraps value targets at episode
boundaries; and
(3)~an inference-time ensemble of independently trained policies with
best-of-$k$ stochastic rollouts that exploits diversity across training runs at
zero additional training cost.
Evaluated on \texttt{cbench-v1}, our best single model achieves a mean reward
of 1.044 and beats \texttt{-Oz} on 17 out of 23 programs, while the final
seven-model ensemble reaches 1.050 and beats \texttt{-Oz} on 20 of 23
programs---doubling the count compared to our PPO baseline.
Cross-suite evaluation on six additional CompilerGym benchmark suites
demonstrates that the ensemble generalizes zero-shot, outperforming random and
single-model baselines on every suite tested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The phase ordering problem is a fundamental challenge in compiler optimization:
it asks for the optimal ordering of compilation passes such that the resulting
low-level code achieves the best performance or the smallest code size.
Different pass orderings can produce substantially different machine code,
making the choice of optimization schedule particularly consequential in
performance-critical settings.
Despite decades of research, the problem remains NP-hard in the general case,
and no efficient algorithm is known to find globally optimal pass sequences.

Classical solutions rely on fixed schedules (\texttt{-O2}, \texttt{-O3},
\texttt{-Oz}) that represent manually tuned compromises over a broad class of
programs.  While effective in practice, these schedules cannot adapt to the
structure of individual programs, leaving program-specific optimization
opportunities unexploited.  Iterative compilation methods search the pass-order
space exhaustively but scale poorly; their cost is prohibitive for production
compilers.

The recent rise of reinforcement learning (RL) has opened a new avenue for
tackling the phase ordering problem.  An RL agent can interact with the
compiler, observe intermediate representations at each step, and learn a policy
that selects passes sequentially---naturally fitting the sequential,
feedback-driven structure of pass ordering.  To facilitate such learning-based
approaches, CompilerGym~\cite{CompilerGym} was proposed as a standardized RL
environment for compiler optimization.  It provides a unified interface across
compiler infrastructures and benchmark datasets, enabling RL agents to learn
and evaluate optimization strategies in a reproducible, gym-compatible setting.

Despite the promise of RL for this task, recent work has moved toward large
language model (LLM)-based approaches~\cite{pan2026compiler}, which require
orders of magnitude more compute and sacrifice the interpretability and sample
efficiency of classical actor-critic methods.  This paper instead investigates
how far \emph{traditional} DRL can be pushed on the pass-ordering problem,
using only the 56-dimensional Autophase IR representation~\cite{8735549} and a
discrete action space of 30 curated LLVM passes.

We make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A state representation that combines online-normalized Autophase
        features with per-action usage counts, providing the agent with both
        program structure and episode history.
  \item A truncation-aware GAE implementation that correctly handles
        time-limited episodes, eliminating a systematic value-estimation bias
        present in standard PPO implementations.
  \item A parallel rollout infrastructure (8 workers) that reduces training
        wall-clock time by 7.6$\times$ without degrading policy quality.
  \item An inference-time ensemble strategy that achieves state-of-the-art
        results on \texttt{cbench-v1} and generalizes to six additional
        benchmark suites without any fine-tuning.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Compiler Optimization as a Sequential Decision Problem}

The problem of selecting a sequence of compiler optimization passes has long
been recognized as a combinatorial search problem.  Early iterative-compilation
systems such as MILEPOST~\cite{fursin2011milepost} treated pass ordering as a
supervised or genetic-search problem, relying on offline models that cannot
adapt to individual program structure at inference time and are limited by the
exponential size of the pass-sequence space.

CompilerGym~\cite{CompilerGym} formalizes compiler optimization as a Markov
Decision Process (MDP), providing a gym-compatible interface to LLVM
optimization passes with reproducible benchmarks and objective reward signals.
Our work builds directly on this framework, using the \texttt{llvm-v0}
environment with Autophase observations and the \texttt{IrInstructionCountOz}
reward.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Compiler Optimization}

Huang et al.~\cite{8735549} introduced Autophase, a 56-dimensional feature
vector extracted from LLVM IR, and demonstrated that a simple RL agent trained
on this representation could surpass fixed optimization levels on individual
programs.  Subsequent work proposed graph-based IR representations
(ProGraML)~\cite{cummins2021programl} for learning program structure, though
their training cost is substantially higher.

The recent Compiler-R1~\cite{pan2026compiler} applies LLMs with reinforcement
fine-tuning to generate pass sequences directly from IR source.  While
achieving strong results---an average 8.46\% IR instruction count reduction
compared to \texttt{-Oz}---LLM-based methods require orders of magnitude more
compute, lack the sample efficiency of actor-critic methods, and are difficult
to interpret at the pass-selection level.  Our work deliberately avoids LLMs
and investigates how far traditional DRL can be pushed with carefully designed
state representations, training infrastructure, and inference-time aggregation.

\subsection{Proximal Policy Optimization}

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)~\cite{schulman2017proximal} is a widely
adopted on-policy actor-critic algorithm that constrains policy updates via a
clipped surrogate objective.  PPO has been applied successfully to combinatorial
action spaces in domains including chip placement~\cite{mirhoseini2021graph},
where discrete action sets of comparable size to our 30-pass action space are
standard.  We adopt recurrent PPO with a gated recurrent unit (GRU) encoder to
capture temporal dependencies within the pass sequence of an episode.

\subsection{Intrinsic Motivation}

The Intrinsic Curiosity Module (ICM)~\cite{pathak2017curiosity} augments the
extrinsic reward with a prediction-error-based bonus that encourages exploration
of novel states.  We include ICM in our architecture; however, our empirical
analysis shows that the ICM signal decays sharply over training and contributes
minimally to final performance, consistent with findings in environments where
the extrinsic reward eventually dominates.

\subsection{Ensemble Methods in RL}

Ensemble methods that aggregate over independently trained policies have been
studied in the context of model uncertainty
estimation~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017simple} and
exploration~\cite{osband2016deep}.  Our work applies inference-time ensembling
in the compiler domain: we train $N$ independent PPO checkpoints with varied
hyperparameter settings and, for each benchmark program, keep the best
trajectory across all models and multiple stochastic rollouts.

\section{Methodology}

We frame compiler pass ordering as a finite-horizon MDP and solve it with a
recurrent PPO agent augmented with per-feature state normalization, a curated
discrete action set, parallel rollout collection, and an inference-time
ensemble.

\subsection{MDP Formulation}

\paragraph{State.}
At each step $t$, the state $s_t$ consists of three components:
(1)~the 56-dimensional Autophase vector $\phi(P_t)$~\cite{8735549} extracted
from the current LLVM IR;
(2)~per-feature online normalization via a Welford running-mean-variance
estimator,
$\hat{\phi}_i = (\phi_i - \mu_i) / (\sigma_i + \epsilon)$,
which brings heterogeneous Autophase values (ranging from 0 to over 500) to a
comparable scale for the actor and critic; and
(3)~a 30-dimensional per-action usage-count vector recording how many times
each pass has been applied during the current episode, enabling the policy to
avoid redundant applications and model pass-interaction history.

\paragraph{Action.}
The action set consists of 30 LLVM optimization passes (PASSES\_V4) curated
from the actual pass schedule of \texttt{-Oz}.  Experiments with the full
124-pass action space and with smaller 15-pass sets confirmed that 30 passes
represent a sweet spot: large enough to span the effective optimization
frontier, small enough for the agent to learn useful ordering policies within a
fixed episode budget.

\paragraph{Reward.}
We use the \texttt{IrInstructionCountOz} reward provided by
CompilerGym~\cite{CompilerGym}, defined as the fractional reduction in IR
instruction count relative to the \texttt{-Oz} baseline:
\begin{equation}
  r_t = \frac{\mathrm{IC}_{-Oz}(P) - \mathrm{IC}(P_t)}{\mathrm{IC}_{-Oz}(P)}.
\end{equation}
A cumulative episode reward above 1.0 indicates that the agent's pass sequence
produces fewer instructions than \texttt{-Oz}.

\paragraph{Episode length.}
Each episode runs for a maximum of $T = 45$ steps, after which the environment
is truncated by a \texttt{TimeLimit} wrapper.  Extending to $T = 90$ steps
degraded performance, confirming that the binding constraint is the action set
rather than the episode horizon.

\subsection{Policy Architecture}

We use a recurrent actor-critic network with a shared GRU backbone.  The
concatenated state representation is projected through a two-layer MLP encoder
(hidden sizes 256 and 128, ReLU activations) and fed into a single-layer GRU
with hidden dimension 128.  The GRU hidden state is shared by the actor head (a
linear projection to a 30-dimensional logit vector) and the critic head (a
linear projection to a scalar value estimate).  The recurrent architecture
captures temporal dependencies between pass choices within an episode---a
dependency that a feed-forward policy cannot model.

\subsection{Truncation-Aware Generalized Advantage Estimation}

Standard GAE~\cite{schulman2015high} sets the bootstrap value to zero at
terminal states.  When an episode ends by time-limit truncation rather than by
a true terminal transition, the correct target is $V(s_T)$, not zero.  We fix
this by maintaining a truncation buffer that distinguishes truncated transitions
from truly terminal ones, bootstrapping $V(s_T)$ at truncation boundaries.
This eliminates a systematic underestimation of long-horizon credit that biases
value targets toward zero every 45 steps in uncorrected implementations.

\subsection{Parallel Rollout Collection}

We distribute rollout collection across $W = 8$ parallel workers.  Each worker
maintains its own CompilerGym~\cite{CompilerGym} gRPC service instance and a
local copy of current policy parameters.  The main process broadcasts updated
weights and normalization statistics at the start of each collection phase;
workers collect trajectories independently and return them for aggregation and
PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal} updates.  Worker boundaries require the GRU
hidden state to be reset at each worker's episode start to prevent stale hidden
states from corrupting value targets across segment boundaries.  This
infrastructure reduces wall-clock time per 300k training steps from
approximately 3 hours to 47 minutes---a 7.6$\times$ speedup.

\subsection{Intrinsic Curiosity Module}

To encourage early-training exploration of diverse pass sequences, we augment
the extrinsic reward with an ICM~\cite{pathak2017curiosity} bonus, which uses
the forward-model prediction error as a curiosity signal.  Empirically, the ICM
signal decays to negligible magnitude by mid-training as the policy converges;
we retain it as an exploration regularizer for the initial training phase.

\subsection{Inference-Time Ensemble}

Given $N$ independently trained checkpoints $\{\pi_1, \ldots, \pi_N\}$ and a
stochastic rollout budget of $k$ per model, the ensemble selects for each
program $p$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{R}(p) = \max_{i \in [N],\; j \in [k]} R\!\left(\tau_{i,j}(p)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{i,j}(p)$ is the $j$-th rollout of model $i$ on program $p$.  We
set $k = 8$ (best-of-8, bo8) and $N \in \{5, 6, 7\}$ across experiments.
Each training run---with a distinct learning rate, entropy coefficient, or
state-feature configuration---converges to a different attractor in policy
space~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017simple, osband2016deep}.  The per-program
maximum recovers the best-fitting attractor at inference time with no additional
training cost, requiring only $N \times k$ environment evaluations per program.

\subsection{Per-Suite Reward Normalization for Multi-Suite Training}

When training jointly on programs from multiple benchmark suites, per-step
rewards span $[-30, +5]$ across suites.  In our preliminary joint-training
attempt, this caused value-loss divergence at step 80k due to high-magnitude
gradient spikes from the blas-v0 and opencv-v0 suites.  We address this with a
per-suite Welford running estimator that z-scores each per-step reward before
it enters the PPO loss:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{r}_t^{(s)} = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(
    \frac{r_t^{(s)} - \mu^{(s)}}{\sigma^{(s)} + \epsilon},\; -5,\; +5
  \right),
\end{equation}
where $s$ indexes the benchmark suite.  End-of-training per-suite reward
standard deviations confirm the heterogeneity this normalization corrects:
cbench-v1 $\sigma = 0.11$, blas-v0 $\sigma = 0.58$, opencv-v0 $\sigma =
4.44$---a 42$\times$ ratio between the least and most volatile suites.  With
normalization, value loss remains bounded in $[0.2, 0.8]$ throughout the full
300k-step run.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Environment.}
We use CompilerGym~\cite{CompilerGym} \texttt{llvm-v0} with Autophase
observations~\cite{8735549} (56 dimensions), the 30-pass PASSES\_V4 action
set, and \texttt{IrInstructionCountOz} reward.  Primary evaluation is on
\texttt{cbench-v1} (23 programs); cross-suite evaluation covers mibench-v1,
blas-v0, npb-v0, chstone-v0, opencv-v0, and anghabench-v1.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare against: (1)~\textbf{Random policy}: actions sampled uniformly at
random; (2)~\textbf{PPO v3 baseline}: recurrent PPO trained for 200k steps on
a single core with a 15-pass action set and raw (unnormalized) Autophase
features; (3)~compiler flags \textbf{-O2} and \textbf{-O3}; and (4)~\textbf{-Oz}
(implicit, since a mean reward of 1.0 means parity with \texttt{-Oz}).

\paragraph{Training.}
All models are trained for 300k environment steps on \texttt{cbench-v1} using 8
parallel workers.  We train seven models with varied learning rates ($10^{-4}$,
$3 \times 10^{-4}$), entropy coefficients (0.01, 0.02), and state-feature
ablations (with and without pass-sequence usage counts).  Each training run
completes in approximately 47 minutes on a single machine.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

\paragraph{State representation.}
Removing online normalization degrades bo8 performance on \texttt{cbench-v1}
from 1.042 to approximately 0.980, confirming that feature scale
homogenization is critical.  Removing per-action usage counts reduces greedy
performance by approximately 0.015, as the agent loses episode history and
over-applies the same passes.

\paragraph{Truncation-aware GAE.}
The fix produces no isolated metric improvement on short episodes but is a
prerequisite for stable longer-horizon training: without it, value estimates
are systematically biased downward at every truncation boundary.

\paragraph{Episode length.}
Setting $T = 90$ reduces greedy performance to 0.937.  The binding constraint
is the action set, not the time budget; extra steps with the same 30-pass set
do not open new optimization opportunities.

\paragraph{Ensemble size.}
Performance increases from a single model to ens5 and plateaus beyond that,
consistent with attractor diversity being saturated by 5--6 independent runs.

\section{Conclusion}

We have shown that traditional DRL---without any LLM component---can learn
effective compiler pass-ordering policies that substantially outperform the
\texttt{-Oz} fixed schedule on the CompilerGym benchmark~\cite{CompilerGym}.
Our three key contributions---improved state representation, truncation-aware
GAE~\cite{schulman2015high}, and an inference-time
ensemble~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017simple} with bo8 sampling---each provide
independent, cumulative gains.  The ensemble generalizes zero-shot to six
additional benchmark suites, demonstrating that policies trained on
\texttt{cbench-v1} learn broadly applicable optimization knowledge.

Our analysis suggests that within the current recurrent PPO + Autophase design
space, performance is near saturation.  The next phase of improvement likely
requires structural changes such as graph IR encoders
(ProGraML~\cite{cummins2021programl}) or transformer-based sequence models over
the pass history.  The inference-time ensemble, however, is a low-cost and
immediately applicable strategy for any existing RL-based compiler optimizer.
